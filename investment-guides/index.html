Investment Guides

One podcast episode on why photography packages stop converting, turned into a build spec for three guides — MTP branding, MTP family sessions, and Pilgrimage Media branding and progress sessions.

01 — The diagnosis

The problem isn't your price

Brooke has reviewed hundreds of photographers' pricing pages looking for why leads don't become bookings, and says the same mistake shows up almost every time.

The mistake is under-communicated value: build a package, slap a bulleted list on it, attach a price, call it done. A bulleted list doesn't sell anything on its own, because the client isn't buying a number of images or a number of hours. They're making a financial decision about an experience.

They open your message on their phone. They see two or three paragraphs, a list of bullet points saying what they get, and a dollar amount underneath each one. That's all the information you're giving them… You have handed them information, but you have not given them a reason to say yes today. Common Pricing Mistake

What follows is predictable. They don't feel anything pulling them toward a decision, so they tell themselves they'll think about it. Then life gets busy, the decision moves to someday, and someday rarely arrives on its own. It reads like being ghosted or undercut on price. Usually it's neither.

The signal to watch. Healthy lead volume, bookings not matching it, and you're confident the photography itself isn't the problem — that combination is almost always pricing and packaging waving a red flag.

It does not automatically mean lower your prices. The fix is often restructuring what's inside each package, or simply clarifying who each one was built for, so the choice feels obvious instead of overwhelming.

02 — The framework

Three things to check

The same three she looks at first whenever a photographer sends pricing over for review — independent of genre.

Check one

Are you showing value, or only telling it?

This is where an investment guide comes in. She's used one since 2018 and still uses the method in 2026 — by her account it converts more leads than a conversation or a phone call ever could, and she sends a Canva view link every single time someone asks about pricing.

A guide lets people see real images from real sessions, and start picturing their own family, their own senior year, their own brand looking that way. It gives them something to feel, not just read.

Ask of each offer When someone asks what you charge, do they get a link to something built — or a price in the body of an email?
Check two

Do they understand what they're getting and why it matters?

Listing location, outfits and image count is efficient, but it only says what's technically included. It never says who the package is built for, or what the whole experience looks like from first step to finished gallery.

Walk them through it like you're explaining it to a friend: what the session feels like from arrival to wrap, what it's like when the gallery lands in their inbox, what kind of client usually picks this one and why it fits their life.

The test Reading your package description, can they picture their own kid, their own living room, their own senior year somewhere in it? If not, it isn't finished.
Check three

Does the pricing actually work?

Less about presentation, more about the numbers underneath. Is each package genuinely profitable once you account for your time and expenses? Is it genuinely valuable to the person on the other end?

And is there real, noticeable separation between tiers, so that moving up feels like a meaningful upgrade rather than a rounding error?

This week Sit down and do a pricing check-in against real data, emotions set to one side.

Her own worked example. She didn't overhaul her senior packages — she added a third option and adjusted the price of each. More seniors now book the second and third tiers, which was the whole point, and she reports profiting an additional $100–200 per booking.

Worth noting the auto-generated transcript renders that figure as "$1 to $200," which is almost certainly a transcription slip for "$100 to $200." The mechanism is the part to borrow regardless: a third tier changes which tier looks like the sensible middle.

03 — Applied

Your four offers

Same framework, four different buyers. The distinction that matters most here: two of these are bought with business money and two with household money, and that changes what "value" has to prove.

MTP

Branding sessions

Buying with
Business money — needs a return, not just a memory.
Real job
Replace a stale headshot and a thin library with images that carry a whole year of marketing.
Value proof
Show the library, not the session. Where the images end up: site header, socials, press, deck.
Tier logic
Scale by breadth of usable library — looks, locations, volume.
MTP

Family sessions

Buying with
Household money — emotional, discretionary, easily deferred.
Real job
Mark a year of a family that won't look like this again.
Value proof
The felt arc: arrival with restless kids, through to opening the gallery. This is the one where "someday" does the most damage.

Scale by session length and what ends up on the wall.

Branding sessions

Buying with
Business money, likely a different tier of client than MTP.

Buying with
Business money, recurring rather than one-off.

04 — The copy

The five beats

Check two in practice. Every package description walks the same five beats; only the specifics change per offer. Write them as if explaining to a friend.

  1. Who this one is built for

    Name them out loud. "A family who wants a shorter, simpler session." "A founder who needs a year of marketing images in one afternoon." If a reader can't self-identify in the first two lines, the rest doesn't get read.

  2. What the lead-up feels like

    What happens between booking and the day: planning, what to wear, location choice, how much you handle for them. This is where nervous buyers are reassured or lost.

  3. What the session itself feels like

    Arrival to wrap-up. Pace, how long, what you do when a toddler melts down or a camera-shy founder freezes. Concrete beats adjectives every time.

  4. What delivery feels like

    The gallery landing in their inbox and what it's like to scroll it the first time. Brooke singles this out specifically — it's the most-skipped beat and the most vivid one.

  5. Why this tier and not the one below

    Say plainly what the step up buys and who it's worth it for. This is the sentence that moves people from tier one to tier two.

Per-offer prompts. Branding: beat 4 isn't sentiment, it's how fast can I start using these and in what sizes. Family: beat 3 carries the whole sale — a parent's real fear is their kids behaving badly. Progress: beat 5 isn't about a tier, it's about the next session, so write it as the case for the series.

06 — The math

The pricing check-in

Check three. Run each tier of each offer through these four rows before the guide gets designed — design makes a weak ladder look convincing, which is the trap.

RowWhat you're testingFails when
Time in Shoot, travel, culling, editing, delivery, admin — the real hours You've only counted the shoot
Cost out Gear, presets, software, prints, second shooter, fuel Per-session costs are buried in annual overhead
Profit per tier What's actually left, per package, per hour Your cheapest tier earns the least per hour — very common
Tier separation Whether stepping up feels like a real upgrade Tiers differ only by image count — that's a spreadsheet, not a decision

The separation test. Cover the prices and read only the "built for" lines. If you can't tell which tier suits which client, neither can they — and no amount of design fixes that. Fix the ladder first.
